Switching from quetiapine modified-release to standard tablets

If you take quetiapine modified-release (MR) tablets, you may need to switch to standard immediate-release (IR) tablets because of a national shortage. This page explains what this means for you and how the switch will happen.

⚠️ Important: Medicine shortage

There is currently a shortage of quetiapine modified-release tablets across the UK. This means your usual once-daily tablets may not always be available. The good news is that standard quetiapine tablets (taken twice daily) are still available and work just as well.

What you need to know

  • Your modified-release (MR) tablets are taken once a day, but standard (IR) tablets are usually taken twice a day
  • Both types of tablets contain the same medicine and work in the same way
  • Your total daily dose will stay the same – you'll just take it in two smaller doses instead of one large dose
  • Standard tablets are readily available, so you should not have problems getting your prescription
  • Your healthcare team will support you through this change

Why do I need to switch?

The Department of Health and Social Care has issued a national alert about shortages of quetiapine modified-release tablets in all strengths (50mg, 150mg, 200mg, 300mg and 400mg). This shortage is expected to continue for some time, with different brands becoming available at different times.

Standard immediate-release quetiapine tablets remain available and can be used instead. Your doctor or pharmacist will talk to you about switching to make sure you can continue your treatment without interruption.

Good to know

Both modified-release and standard quetiapine tablets contain the same active medicine. Clinical studies have shown they work equally well when taken at the same total daily dose. You should not notice any difference in how your medicine works after switching.

What's the difference between the tablets?

Modified-release (MR) tablets

  • Take once a day (usually in the evening)
  • Release the medicine slowly throughout the day
  • Currently in short supply

Standard immediate-release (IR) tablets

  • Take twice a day (morning and evening)
  • Release the medicine quickly
  • Readily available

Even though you'll be taking your medicine twice a day instead of once, the total amount of medicine you receive each day will be exactly the same.

Your dose conversion

Use this table to understand how your current dose will change. Your total daily dose stays the same – it's just split into two doses.

Your current MR dose (once daily) Your new IR dose (twice daily)
50mg once a day 25mg twice a day (morning and night)
100mg once a day 50mg twice a day (morning and night)
150mg once a day 75mg twice a day (morning and night)
200mg once a day 100mg twice a day (morning and night)
300mg once a day 150mg twice a day (morning and night)
400mg once a day 200mg twice a day (morning and night)
600mg once a day 300mg twice a day (morning and night)

💊 Example

If you currently take 300mg once a day (modified-release), you will switch to 150mg twice a day (standard tablets). That's 150mg in the morning and 150mg in the evening – still 300mg total each day.

How will the switch happen?

Your healthcare team will guide you through the change. Here's what to expect:

  1. Final MR dose: Take your last modified-release tablet as usual at your normal time
  2. Wait 24 hours: Skip your usual dose the next day (wait about 24 hours after your last MR tablet)
  3. Start IR tablets: Begin taking your standard tablets at the new twice-daily schedule, starting with your morning dose
  4. Continue as normal: Keep taking your tablets twice daily – once in the morning and once in the evening
Setting reminders

Because you'll be taking your medicine twice a day instead of once, it may help to set phone reminders or use a pill organiser to make sure you don't miss a dose. Taking your tablets at the same times each day (for example, with breakfast and with dinner) can help you remember.

What should I watch out for?

Most people switch from modified-release to standard tablets without any problems. However, it's important to pay attention to how you feel, especially in the first week or two after switching.

Contact your doctor or pharmacist if you notice:

  • Changes in how well your medicine is working
  • New or worsening side effects
  • Problems with your sleep
  • Feeling more drowsy than usual, especially in the morning
  • Feeling dizzy when you stand up
  • Any other concerns about your medicine

Your healthcare team may need to adjust the timing of your doses (for example, giving you more of your dose in the evening) if you experience side effects.

📞 When to get help

If you have any concerns after switching, or if you're struggling to remember to take your tablets twice a day, contact your doctor, pharmacist, or mental health team. They can help you find solutions that work for you.

Will I need to switch back?

Once the shortage of modified-release tablets is resolved, your doctor may discuss whether to switch you back. However, many people continue well on standard tablets, so you may not need to switch back at all.

If you find that standard tablets work well for you and you have no problems taking them twice a day, there may be no need to change back even when modified-release tablets become available again.

Important reminders

Do not stop your medicine

If you cannot get your usual tablets because of the shortage, do not stop taking quetiapine. Contact your doctor or pharmacist immediately to discuss alternatives. Stopping suddenly could cause your symptoms to return or worsen.

Take as prescribed

Always take your tablets exactly as your doctor has prescribed. Do not change your dose or stop taking your medicine without talking to your healthcare team first.

Keep taking your medicine

Even though you're switching to a different type of tablet, it's the same medicine and it's just as important to keep taking it regularly as prescribed.

Questions to ask your doctor or pharmacist

  • What exact dose should I take in the morning and evening?
  • What times of day should I take my tablets?
  • Should I take them with food?
  • What should I do if I forget to take a dose?
  • Who should I contact if I have problems after switching?
  • Will I need any extra appointments or check-ups?
